Abstract 

We propose a stochastic optimization framework in which football performance is modeled as the result of an efficiency-driven production process under uncertainty. The model incorpo-rates economic inputs, a latent efficiency variable evolving according to a stochastic differential equation, and a control variable representing investment in sports development. We derive the associated discounted Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman (HJB) equation, characterize the optimal in-vestment policy, prove a verification theorem under explicit growth conditions, and show that a bounded investment budget is required for the control problem to be well posed. We relate our efficiency measure to the stochastic frontier and data envelopment analysis (SFA/DEA) traditions used elsewhere in sports economics, and clarify what a dynamic control formulation adds relative to those static approaches. The theoretical results are illustrated with a numerical solution of the HJB equation – validated by a grid-refinement convergence study – and Monte Carlo simulations. A cross-sectional analysis of the 24 AFCON 2025 national teams, based on GDP, population, and FIFA ranking data, estimates country-specific conditional log-efficiency residuals using heteroskedasticity-robust inference. We show that these residuals are mechani-cally and strongly correlated with FIFA points by construction, quantify this dependence, and interpret the results accordingly. Teams such as Senegal and Morocco consistently outperform their GDP-implied potential, whereas others, including South Africa and Tanzania, tend to underperform. While these patterns are broadly consistent with observed AFCON outcomes, the estimated residual also captures unobserved factors, such as institutional quality, diaspora talent, and demographic structure, that are not included in the model. 

Keywords. economic capacity, football performance, African national teams, stochastic opti-mization, uncertainty, stochastic differential equation, stochastic frontier analysis. 

# 1 Introduction 

This research examines the performance of African football by considering three main dimensions: economic resources (budgets, infrastructure, investments), sporting efficiency (the ability to trans-late available resources into sporting results), and the uncertainty associated with competitions (unpredictable events in matches, injuries, and unpredictable performances). Using a stochastic 1arXiv:2610.06967v1 [math.OC] 3 Oct 2026 optimization model, the study seeks to determine how national federations can effectively allocate resources to maximize performance while managing the risks and uncertainties inherent in the sport. The relationship between economic development and sporting performance has long been de-bated in sports economics [Szymanski, 2003, Andreff, 2011]. While wealthier nations are often expected to perform better due to greater access to resources, empirical evidence from African football suggests a more complex reality: Manuel Luiz and Fadal [2011] document that sports performance in Africa is better explained relative to GDP per capita than by GDP levels alone, and Batarfi and Reade [2021] show that, globally, institutional quality and domestic footballing structures matter alongside GDP per capita in explaining national team performance. In the context of the Africa Cup of Nations (AFCON), several countries with relatively modest economic indicators consistently outperform larger economies. This observation raises a fundamen-tal question: is football performance primarily driven by economic capacity, or by the efficiency with which resources are transformed into sporting outcomes? This paper proposes a stochastic optimization framework to address this question. Rather than assuming a direct deterministic relationship between economic variables and performance, we model performance as the outcome of: a production function based on economic inputs, a latent efficiency factor, and stochastic shocks reflecting the inherent uncertainty of sport. The paper makes five contributions. First, we position the efficiency measure used throughout the paper relative to the stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) and data envelopment analysis (DEA) traditions that are the standard tools for measuring efficiency in sports economics (Section 2.4). In addition, we are explicit about what a dynamic control formulation adds – and does not add – relative to those static methods. Second, we specify the efficiency dynamics directly as a controlled process for the log-efficiency variable, derive the associated discounted Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation and the resulting optimal investment rule, and prove a verification theorem under explicit growth conditions (Section 3). Third, we show that the control problem requires a bounded invest-ment budget to be well posed, and that the constrained value function can be validated by explicit finite-difference solution together with a grid-refinement convergence study anchored in the Barles– Souganidis convergence criteria (Section 5). Fourth, we provide transparent statistical inference for the cross-sectional estimation step, including heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and a joint significance test with N = 24 (Sections 4 and 6), and we explicitly quantify the extent to which the estimated efficiency residual mechanically reproduces the raw FIFA ranking (Section 6.1.2). Fifth, we illustrate the model numerically, both by solving the HJB equation on a grid and by Monte Carlo simulation, and further with a cross-sectional empirical exercise on the 24 national teams that qualified for AFCON 2025 (Morocco), using GDP, population, and FIFA ranking data. The article is organized as follows. Section 2 sets out the economic and stochastic building blocks and positions the model relative to SFA/DEA. Section 3 formulates and solves the stochastic control problem, including the verification theorem. Section 4 outlines the estimation strategy and its inferential requirements. Section 5 reports the numerical illustration and the convergence study. Section 6 presents the cross-sectional empirical results, the circularity diagnostic, and the discussion. Finally, in Section 7, we present conclusions and perspectives.

2.4 Relation to stochastic frontier analysis and DEA 

Measuring “efficiency” as the capacity to transform resources into results is precisely the object of stochastic frontier analysis [SFA; Aigner et al., 1977] and of data envelopment analysis [DEA; Charnes et al., 1978], both of which are widely used in sports economics. It is therefore important to state clearly how the present framework relates to these standard tools, and what it adds. 

What SFA and DEA do. SFA estimates a composed-error model yi = xiβ + vi − ui, where 

vi is two-sided noise and ui ≥ 0 is a one-sided inefficiency term with an assumed distribution (half-normal, exponential, truncated normal), separately identified from noise via maximum likelihood. DEA constructs a non-parametric, piecewise-linear best-practice frontier by linear programming and measures each unit’s distance to that frontier, with no distributional assumption but no explicit noise term either. Both methods are static : they deliver a single efficiency score (or ranking) per cross-sectional unit at a point in time, estimated from a snapshot of inputs and outputs. 

What the residual in this paper is – and is not. The empirical residual zi that we estimate by OLS in Section 6 (the log of observed performance net of the fitted GDP/population potential) is a symmetric residual, not a one-sided SFA inefficiency term: it can be positive or negative, it embeds ordinary measurement noise together with any true efficiency component, and no distributional assumption separates the two. It is, in this precise sense, closer to a corrected-OLS (COLS) shift of the fitted frontier than to an SFA-consistent decomposition, and considerably simpler than DEA. We are explicit about this throughout the paper (see Section 6.1.2), and we refer to zi as a conditional (log-)efficiency residual – performance conditional on GDP and population – rather than as a structurally identified efficiency parameter. 3What the HJB/control framework adds. The contribution of this paper is not a better 

static efficiency estimator than SFA or DEA. It is a framework for what happens after an efficiency level – however estimated – is observed: a stochastic control problem in which a federation chooses a time path of investment ut to move a stochastic, mean-reverting efficiency state θt toward a higher long-run level, subject to a quadratic adjustment cost and a bounded budget, deriving a normative, forward-looking optimal investment rule (Section 3) rather than a backward-looking efficiency score. SFA and DEA answer “how efficient was unit i last period, relative to the frontier?”; the control problem answers “given a current efficiency state (however measured), how much should be invested, and how does that investment interact with mean reversion and volatility over time?”. The two are complementary rather than competing: a natural extension, noted in Section 7, is to feed a properly identified SFA (or panel COLS) efficiency estimate into the state zi, 0 of the dynamic model, in place of the simple cross-sectional OLS residual used here for illustration.

3.4 Well-posedness: why a budget cap is needed 

Equation (5) contains a reward term ez ft that is convex and unbounded in z, together with a control benefit γuW z that is only quadratically penalized. If u is left unconstrained, the feedback

3.6 Interpretation 

• Investment increases the drift of log-efficiency, hence (in expectation) future performance. 

• Because performance is exponential (multiplicative) in z, the marginal value of investment 

Wz – and hence optimal investment itself – is generically increasing in current efficiency (Sec-tion 5): efficiency and investment reinforce each other, consistent with cumulative-advantage dynamics in institutional quality. 

• Costs and the budget cap limit excessive investment. 

• Uncertainty ( σ) affects the dispersion, not the mean-reversion target, of outcomes. This is a finite-horizon stochastic optimal control problem with a nonlinear (quadratic in Wz )HJB equation; a closed-form solution is not available in general because the exponential forcing term and the linear-in-z drift term do not close under a low-dimensional ansatz. Analytical approx-imation techniques such as those of Dixit [1991] (local quadratic expansions around ¯ z) could deliver an approximate closed form for future work; here we solve Equation (5) numerically (Section 5).

5.2 Optimal investment policy 

Figure 1 plots u∗(z, t ) at several dates. Two features stand out. First, at any given date, u∗ is increasing in z: because performance is exponential in log-efficiency, the marginal value of raising efficiency further is itself larger when efficiency is already high, so optimal investment is directed disproportionately toward already-efficient federations – a form of increasing returns to institutional investment, rather than the “catch-up” pattern one might have guessed a priori. Second, u∗ declines toward zero as t → T : with little time left to capture the benefits of higher efficiency, investment is not worthwhile close to the terminal date, the usual turnpike/vanishing-effort feature of finite-horizon control problems.

Rather than treating this residual as direct evidence of an efficiency channel, Section 6.1.2 below quantifies exactly how much of that residual variation is, by construction, simply a repackaging of the raw FIFA ranking. We take ¯ zi = zi as each country’s current structural log-efficiency level and fi = exp( dln f (GDP i, P op i)) as its structural GDP/population-based potential, and simulate Equation (3) forward from zi, 0 = ¯ zi

with common structural parameters γ = 1, c = 1, and identical constant investment u = 0 .2 for every country over T = 5 years. We flag explicitly that setting ¯ zi = zi – i.e., treating each country’s currently estimated residual as its long-run structural target – is an identification assumption , not an innocuous normalization: it is what allows us to simulate forward from a single cross-section without a panel estimate of ¯ zi, and it mechanically preserves each country’s current relative stand-ing rather than allowing the model to independently discover it. A genuine panel estimate of ¯ zi

(Step 2 of Section 4) is needed to relax this assumption; see Section 7. 

An attempted calibration of κ, σ , and why it was not used. We attempted to calibrate the shared dynamic parameters κ (mean-reversion speed) and σ (volatility) to real data, using veri-fied AFCON results (podium finishes and quarter-finalists, cross-checked against official records) for the last four 24-team editions (2019, 2021, 2023, 2025). Identifying κ, σ from an AR(1)/Ornstein– Uhlenbeck regression requires country-level observations in two or more consecutive editions, but the great majority of the 24 countries have an independently verified result (top-4 finish or quarter-final exit) in only one of the four editions – the remaining cells would have to be filled in from unverified recollection rather than sourced results. With only a handful of usable consecutive pairs, a regression-based ˆ κ, ˆσ would be dominated by sampling noise rather than signal, so we do not report one: a spurious point estimate presented as “calibrated” would be worse than being explicit about the limitation. Genuinely identifying κi, σ i requires the kind of multi-cycle panel (AFCON points and time-varying GDP, several editions deep, for every country) that the Kalman-filter strategy of Step 2, Section 4, is built for; assembling that panel is future work. Here we instead run 

κ, σ at an illustrative baseline ( κ = 0 .7, σ = 0 .30) and directly check, below, how much the results depend on that choice. 

6.1.2 Quantifying circularity in the empirical exercise 

With ˆ α1 = 0 .061 and ˆ α2 = 0 .006, the fitted production function is close to flat in the two economic regressors, so the residual zi is close to a re-centered version of ln(FIFA i). We quantify this explicitly rather than leave it implicit. By construction of the OLS residual, Corr( zi, ln(FIFA i)) = √1 − R2;with R2 = 0 .347 this gives Corr  zi, ln(FIFA i) = √1 − 0.347 = 0 .808 ,

which we also confirm numerically from the estimated residuals (0 .8082). In other words, roughly 1 − R2 ≈ 65% of the cross-country variance in log FIFA points is, mechanically, still present in the “efficiency” residual zi. This is not a substantive empirical finding about football; it is an algebraic identity that holds for any regression with this R2, and it means that a ranking of countries by zi

12 will necessarily look similar to a ranking by raw FIFA points. Consistent with this, the Spearman rank correlation between the country ranking implied by zi and the raw FIFA-points ranking is 0.734 ( p < 0.001). We draw two conclusions from this. First, statements of the form “Senegal and Morocco out-perform their GDP-implied potential” should be read, and are reported throughout this paper, as conditional on the weak explanatory power of the fitted production function : given how little of FIFA points GDP and population jointly explain ( R2 = 0 .35), a claim that country i has high 

zi is close to, though not identical to, the more mundane claim that country i has a high FIFA ranking. Second, and more constructively, this is precisely why we relabel zi throughout this paper as a conditional performance residual (performance conditional on GDP and population) rather than as a validated structural efficiency parameter (Section 2.4): the residual almost certainly also absorbs omitted structural factors that are correlated with football performance but not modeled here – diaspora and expatriate players, football tradition, institutional quality [Batarfi and Reade, 2021], and working-age male population (15–35) rather than total population. Because we do not have reliable cross-country data on these factors for the present sample, we do not add them as additional controls in this version of the paper; instead we are explicit about the limitation here and in Section 7, and we requalify zi accordingly everywhere it is used, including in the abstract.

Remarks. This exercise uses FIFA ranking points, not AFCON tournament points, as the performance measure Yi, and a single cross-section rather than a panel, so zi should be read as “footballing strength relative to GDP and population, as of mid-2026,” and, per Section 6.1.2, as a residual that mechanically correlates with raw FIFA points at ρ = 0 .81; and γ, c , and (per the discussion above) κ, σ , are shared across countries and set at illustrative values rather than estimated individually. It nonetheless directly operationalizes, for the first time in this paper, the mechanism the theoretical model was built to capture, and the sensitivity analysis shows its central ranking result is not an artifact of the un-identified parameters. 

6.3 Discussion 

The model and its numerical illustration yield several insights: 

• Economic resources determine potential, not performance: f (Xi,t ) sets the scale, but θi,t 

determines how much of that potential is realized. 

• Efficiency is the key driver of success, and, because performance is multiplicative in effi-ciency, gains in efficiency compound: the optimal policy directs more investment toward already-efficient federations (Section 5), which helps explain the persistence of over- and under-performance relative to GDP across cycles. 

• Stochastic shocks explain variability in outcomes; volatility σi widens the distribution of possible results without shifting the long-run target ¯ zi.16 • Optimal investment policies depend on structural parameters ( κi, γ i, c i) and on the remaining time horizon, and are only well defined once a realistic investment budget cap is imposed. 

• The conditional efficiency residual used in Section 6 is informative but should not be over-read: roughly two-thirds of its cross-country variance is, by construction, a restatement of the raw performance ranking (Section 6.1.2), and the remainder still mixes any genuine efficiency effect with omitted structural factors (diaspora, institutional quality, demographic structure) that are not separately controlled for here. In particular, the cross-country illustration of Section 5 shows, in a controlled numerical setting, that a country with moderate GDP but high efficiency can outperform a richer, less efficient country, and the 24-country cross-section of Section 6 shows a broadly consistent pattern in real GDP, population, and FIFA ranking data for the actual AFCON 2025 field – Senegal and Morocco substantially over-perform their economic potential, while South Africa, despite the largest GDP in the sample, and Tanzania and Zimbabwe under-perform theirs. This is qualitatively consistent with the empirical evidence in Manuel Luiz and Fadal [2011], Batarfi and Reade [2021], though, as emphasized throughout, the strength of that consistency is bounded by the weak explanatory power ( R2 = 0 .35) of the underlying production-function regression. The model suggests that: 

• Improving governance and training systems is more effective than increasing budgets alone – as efficiency rises, increasingly, early institutional gains are worth protecting and building on. 

• Strategic investment in football infrastructure yields long-term efficiency gains, but is subject to a genuine budget constraint: unlimited investment is not just unrealistic but, absent a cap, is not even well defined as an optimization problem (Section 3). 

• Stability (low σi) reduces volatility and improves the reliability, though not necessarily the mean level, of expected performance. 

# 7 Conclusion and future work 

This paper proposes a stochastic optimization framework for understanding football performance in Africa. By modeling efficiency as a controlled stochastic process, deriving and verifying the associated Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation under a discounted objective, and positioning the resulting efficiency measure explicitly relative to the stochastic frontier and DEA traditions, we pro-vide a theoretical explanation for the observed disconnect between economic capacity and sporting success, together with a normative optimal investment rule. We show that a bounded investment budget is required for well-posedness, give a verification theorem under explicit growth conditions, validate the numerical solution by a grid-refinement convergence study, and illustrate the resulting optimal policy and its cross-country implications by simulation. Applied to real data for the 24 AFCON 2025 national teams, the cross-sectional exercise reproduces the paper’s central qualita-tive claim – Senegal and Morocco over-perform their GDP-implied potential, while South Africa, Tanzania, and Zimbabwe under-perform theirs – while being explicit that roughly two-thirds of the underlying residual’s cross-country variance is, by construction, a restatement of the raw FIFA ranking, and that the residual still absorbs factors not modeled here. Future work may extend this framework to: 

• a genuine multi-cycle panel estimate of ( κi, σ i, ¯zi) (AFCON tournament points, and time-varying GDP, several editions deep, for every country) via the Kalman-filter strategy of 17 Section 4, which alone can identify country-specific dynamic parameters and relax the ¯ zi = zi

identification assumption used in Section 6; 

• additional controls for the factors most likely to be absorbed into the current residual – diaspora and expatriate-player data, an institutional-quality index, and working-age (15–35) male population in place of total population – together with a properly specified stochastic frontier (SFA) decomposition of noise versus one-sided inefficiency, to reduce reliance on the symmetric-residual shortcut used here (Sections 2.4 and 6.1.2); 

• player-level data and network effects (diaspora) and dynamic tournament modeling with strategic interaction between federations; 

• analytical approximations to the HJB equation (e.g., local quadratic expansions in the spirit of Dixit [1991]) to complement the numerical solution.
